Johnston’s problem 14

Mertens’ first theorem

How far must one go before the sum of (log p)/p falls below its main term? Follow the proof from Büthe’s computation to an interval on which the error stays negative.

Definition of the error
E₁(x) = Σp ≤ x (log p)/p − log x − E,
E = −γ − Σp (log p)/[p(p − 1)].

The sums run over primes. Here γ is Euler’s constant.

How the question arose

The author’s work on stability and on Sendov’s problem led to a question about changing weights. Two prime sums involve the same zeta zeros. How much of the sign margin already found by Büthe for one sum will survive in the other? The paper answers this by bounding the change of coefficients and the contribution of prime powers.

Upper bound for a negative valueX < 1.91 × 10215The first sign change may occur earlier.

Appendix A.2

Where does the term at 7² go?

Follow a single prime power pk across the cutoff. In A(x) its weight is counted as soon as pk ≤ x. In the tail P(x) the same weight is counted while pk > x.

Why do the prime squares contribute nearly one?

Write y = √x. The scaled square tail is T(y) = y Σp > y (log p)/p². Each prime adds log p to the step function ϑ(t) at t = p. This turns the tail into 2y ∫y∞ (ϑ(t) − ϑ(y))/t³ dt. Replacing ϑ(t) by t gives 2y ∫y∞ (t − y)/t³ dt = 1 for every y.

Appendix B.1–B.3

What happens to one oscillation?

The mean in the proof looks only at a narrow window around ω. First see how much of its weight lies near the centre. Then follow one frequency as the edges of the window are smoothed, or compare the two reciprocal expressions used to bound the change in its coefficient.

Appendix B.1 · Watch the two poles cancel

Take x = 10 and write r = 1 + d. The pole term is −10−d/d. From the logarithmic derivative of ζ we get 1/d − γE + O(d). Add the terms containing 1/d before taking the limit.

From one frequency to the zero sum · Appendix B.1–B.3

Landau’s formula first applies to smooth tests. Appendix B.1 cancels the two pole terms. It then uses the symmetry ρ ↔ 1 − ρ to express the contribution of each zero with the same sign convention as Büthe’s sum.

Appendix B.2 first translates the kernel to ω. For ρ = ½ + iγ the translation contributes a phase of modulus one, and the centred kernel contributes the real factor ℓ(γ). Smoothing multiplies this contribution by a second factor Md(ρ), which tends to 1 for each fixed zero. The two absolute tail estimates and the bound |Md(ρ)| ≤ exp(d/2) justify the same limiting step for the entire sum.

Appendix B.3 uses nonnegativity and unit mass to get |ℓ(γ)| ≤ 1. On the critical line this bounds the change of coefficient for a single zero by 1/(ω|ρ|²), the quantity that is summed in the sign budget.

Appendix C.2 · Figure 3

How far left does the sign persist?

Start at the unknown witness x₀ and move left to t = x₀ − 10⁻⁶Q√x₀. Crossing a prime can only lower the prime sum. The rise allowed by the logarithm gives an upper envelope for 10⁶√x₀ E₁(t).

Why the envelope is positive at 60.50

Write D = 10⁶ exp(495.702833109/2). The paper gives U(Q) = −60.5 + Q/(1 − Q/D). Rearranging gives

U(Q) = (Q − 60.5) + Q²/(D − Q).

At 60.50 the first term is exactly zero and the second is positive, about 8.38 × 10⁻¹¹¹. The dot looks level with the axis at this resolution. A positive upper bound leaves the sign of the actual error open.

Appendix C.2 · Figure 3. Since U increases with Q, a negative upper bound at the selected distance gives negativity at every intermediate point. At Q = 60 this covers more than 2.6 × 10¹⁰³ ordinary x-units.
